Bear market survival

The page to open when the market is crashing

Bookmark this now, while you're calm. A 30-year plan will live through four to six bear markets, and each one will feel like the one that's finally different. This page exists for those days: the full record of what crashes have done, and the short list of what to do about them.

The record

Every major US crash, and what happened next

The pattern repeats: a decline that felt world-ending at the time, followed by a recovery that in hindsight looks inevitable. The table shows the S&P 500's major declines on a price basis (dividends reinvested shortened every recovery below, in some cases by years):

Read the table twice. Once for the declines — that column is why your allocation must match your stomach. Then again for the recovery column: every single crash in a century ended the same way, with the market at new highs. The people hurt permanently were almost never the ones who held; they were the ones who sold at the bottom, or held leverage and never got the chance to decide.
The playbook

What to actually do in a crash

Do

  • Nothing, mostly. If your plan was right last month, a lower price doesn't make it wrong. Doing nothing is an active, correct decision.
  • Let the automation run. Your monthly buy is now purchasing more shares per unit of currency than it did at the peak. This is the mechanism working, not failing.
  • Re-read your investment policy — the one-page document you wrote when calm (see Principles). It was written precisely for today.
  • Rebalance if your bands say so. A crash pushes stock weight below target; rebalancing then means buying low on a rule, not on a feeling.
  • Check your cash runway, not your portfolio. The question that matters is "can I avoid selling for the next year or two?" — not "how much is it down today?"

Don't

  • Don't sell "until things settle down". Recovery days cluster inside the chaos; missing a handful of them costs a large share of lifetime returns.
  • Don't check prices daily. Nothing in a 2050 plan requires today's quote. Every check is another chance to panic.
  • Don't reach for leverage or "buy the dip" money you may need. Crashes get deeper than feels possible; only invest cash with a decade-long runway.
  • Don't read forecasts. Nobody called the bottom in 1932, 1974, 2009, or 2020. The confident voices on the way down were guessing too.
  • Don't confuse your market with every market. A crash in one country is a reminder to stay diversified, not a reason to abandon the plan.
Why holding works

The arithmetic of panic

Three facts, all covered in depth on the Principles page, do the heavy lifting:

  • The best days live inside the worst months. Studies of the S&P 500 repeatedly find that missing only the 10 best days across decades roughly halves final wealth — and those days overwhelmingly occurred during bear markets, next to the worst days.
  • A falling market raises the return on every new contribution. For anyone still adding money monthly, a bear market is the sale of the decade running in slow motion. The investors who compounded through 2008–09 or March 2020 bought their best-performing shares then.
  • Declines are temporary for broad markets, permanent only for the leveraged and the concentrated. Individual companies go to zero; the S&P 500 never has. That asymmetry is the entire case for owning the broad market instead of a handful of names.
The honest exception. "It always comes back" has one documented failure mode: a single market bought at bubble valuations — Japan needed 34 years (see the Japan case study). The insurance is the same as always: broad diversification, steady buying rather than one lump at a peak, and no leverage. A global or US-broad portfolio bought monthly has no historical precedent for permanent loss over 20+ years. That is a statement about history, not a guarantee — but it is the strongest base rate in investing.
Before the next one

Crash-proof the plan while the sun is out

  1. Hold 3–6 months of expenses in cash. A job loss during a bear market is the one scenario that forces selling at the bottom. The emergency fund exists so the portfolio never has to be one.
  2. Choose an allocation whose worst year you can watch. Rough rule: your stock percentage, halved, is a loss you should expect at least once a decade. 80% stocks → be ready to see −40% on a statement without selling.
  3. Write the policy now. One page: what you own, why, what you will do at −20% and −40% (typically: nothing, or rebalance into stocks). Signed and dated. Future-you will not believe past-you was so calm.
  4. Automate everything so that continuing correctly requires no courage — and stopping requires effort.
  5. Pre-decide your information diet: in a crash, check the portfolio monthly at most, and let the annual review (see Strategy) stay annual.
