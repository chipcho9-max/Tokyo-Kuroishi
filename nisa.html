Japan

NISA — two quotas, one lifetime cap

Japan's reformed NISA is one of the most generous retail tax wrappers anywhere: gains and dividends inside it are tax-free with no time limit, against 20.315% in an ordinary account. But two things are routinely misunderstood — the difference between the tsumitate and growth quotas, and how the ¥18M lifetime cap actually counts. This page is built around those two, then the traps.

Rules move. The figures below reflect the system as of early 2026, simplified for education. Japan's tax code is revised annually. Verify with the FSA's NISA pages (金融庁), the NTA (国税庁), or your broker before acting.

Tsumitate quota vs. growth quota

The biggest misconception is that you pick one. Under the old system (general NISA vs. tsumitate NISA) you did — but the 2024 reform lets you use both in the same year. What actually differs is how much goes in annually, and what you may buy.

Don't be misled by the name. "Growth quota" sounds like it is reserved for aggressive investing. It isn't: you can set up automatic monthly purchases of the very same index fund inside the growth quota. Investing more than ¥100,000 a month (the tsumitate quota's monthly maximum) into one boring index fund is an entirely ordinary way to use both quotas.
The mechanism

The ¥18M cap counts book value, not market value

Understanding this one rule explains the whole design. The lifetime cap is measured at acquisition cost, not current value:

  • Gains never consume the cap. Once you have contributed ¥18M, the holdings can grow to ¥50M and you keep every yen of it inside the wrapper, tax-free, indefinitely. Nothing forces you out. This is what "unlimited holding period" really buys you.
  • Selling restores the cap the following year — at book value. Sell a position you bought for ¥5M that is now worth ¥8M, and what comes back next year is ¥5M of capacity, not ¥8M.
  • Restored capacity doesn't raise the annual limit. You still repurchase within that year's ¥3.6M (¥1.2M + ¥2.4M).

How the ¥18M splits

The growth quota is capped at ¥12M of the ¥18M, so at least ¥6M must come through the tsumitate quota.

The reverse is not true: the tsumitate quota can fill all ¥18M by itself. Never touching the growth quota is perfectly valid — and for a pure index investor it is arguably the default.

What the exemption is worth

¥18M compounding at 7% for 25 years reaches roughly ¥97.7M, of which about ¥79.7M is gain.

In a taxable account, realising that gain costs 20.315% — about ¥16.2M. Inside NISA it is zero. That single fact outweighs most stock-picking skill.

7% is a historical-average assumption, not a promise.

But don't force the pace. This table argues for filling faster with money you already have spare — never for raiding the emergency fund or borrowing to do it. Set the pace from what is left after the emergency fund and high-interest debt are handled. Someone who sustains ¥50,000 a month for 30 years beats someone who manages ¥300,000 a month for two years and quits.
Misconceptions

Seven things people get wrong about NISA

"You have to choose between the tsumitate and growth quotas."

True under the old system, false since 2024. Both run together, for a combined ¥3.6M a year.

"You can't make scheduled contributions in the growth quota."

You can. The name misleads: monthly automatic purchases of index funds work inside the growth quota, and every major online broker supports it. This is the standard route for anyone investing more than ¥100,000 a month.

"The ¥18M cap is market value, so gains push me out of it."

It is book value. Contribute ¥18M, watch it triple, and your usage still reads ¥18M — all of it staying tax-free with no deadline.

"If I sell, the capacity comes back immediately."

It returns the following year, at book value, and repurchases still fit inside that year's ¥3.6M annual limit. The design deliberately does not support frequent trading.

"Losses inside NISA can offset gains elsewhere."

They cannot. This is NISA's biggest weakness. Losses in a NISA account are treated as if they never happened: no offsetting against taxable-account gains, no carry-forward. Gains go untaxed, and losses go unrelieved — the two sides of the same rule.

"US dividends are completely tax-free inside NISA."

Japanese tax (20.315%) disappears, but the 10% withheld in the US remains — and because NISA income isn't taxed in Japan, the foreign tax credit that would rescue it in a taxable account is unavailable. A small, certain cost on dividend-heavy US holdings.

"Reinvesting a fund's distributions doesn't use up capacity."

It does. A distribution reinvested inside NISA counts as a new purchase and consumes that much capacity. This is exactly why non-distributing (internally reinvesting) index funds fit NISA best.

Practical traps

Six administrative details worth money

1. Set dividends to "proportional allocation"

For Japan-listed stocks, ETFs and REITs held in NISA, dividends are only tax-free if your receipt method is 株式数比例配分方式 (paid into the brokerage account). Leave it on bank transfer or post-office receipt and your dividends get taxed 20.315% despite sitting in NISA. This is the single most common way people quietly lose the benefit — check the setting once.

2. Credit-card contributions cap at ¥100,000/mo

Most online brokers award points on fund purchases charged to a credit card, up to ¥100,000 a month (raised from ¥50,000 in March 2024). That lines up exactly with the tsumitate quota's monthly maximum. Point rates change often — check current terms.

3. Changing brokers is an annual decision

You may hold one NISA account at a time. Switching brokers is possible yearly, but once you have bought anything in a given year, the switch takes effect from the next year. Paperwork takes weeks, so start early. Holdings at the old broker stay tax-free where they are — they are not transferred.

4. Old NISA sits in a separate bucket

Anything bought under the pre-2024 general or tsumitate NISA is outside the new ¥18M cap and keeps running to the end of its own exemption period (5 years for general, 20 for tsumitate). It cannot be rolled into the new system, and there is no reason to sell early — just let it run out.

5. Favour non-distributing, low-cost funds

Distributions consume capacity when reinvested (misconception 7). For decades-long index investing, a low-cost all-country or S&P 500 fund that reinvests internally fits NISA's design best. The fund-selection reasoning lives in Strategy.

6. Consider iDeCo alongside it

NISA contributions are not deductible; iDeCo contributions are fully deductible from income. For stable earners, running both adds an annual income- and residence-tax saving on top of tax-free growth — at the cost of a lock-up until 60. See Accounts & Tax.

The honest view

What NISA is bad at

This site does not market government programs. NISA is exceptionally favourable for long-term investors, but three weaknesses deserve to be understood in advance:

  • No loss offsetting or carry-forward. A loss inside NISA is simply lost, taxwise. Concentrated bets and short-term trading inside NISA forfeit both the upside benefit and the usual tax relief. The rational conclusion: NISA is the box for holdings you expect to win over decades, not for speculation.
  • The 10% US withholding is unavoidable. The wrapper is an imperfect fit for dividend-focused US strategies. Check this before putting a high-yield US ETF inside it.
  • Capacity recycles only once a year. Sell-then-refill-next-year makes NISA poor for tactical trading. Turned around, though, the design itself rewards holding — which, for the readers of this site, is a feature rather than a flaw.
Plan the exit too. NISA has no forced withdrawals (no equivalent of US RMDs), so in the spending phase it is normally the last account you touch — every extra year inside it is another year of tax-free compounding. Withdrawal order by country is covered in Drawdown.
Summary

NISA checklist

  1. Use both quotas — they are not alternatives. ¥3.6M a year combined.
  2. Fill the tsumitate quota first — up to ¥100,000 a month. For most people that is the whole plan.
  3. Beyond that, buy the same fund in the growth quota — ignore what the name implies.
  4. Set dividend receipt to proportional allocation — mandatory check if you hold Japan-listed stocks or ETFs.
  5. Prefer non-distributing, low-cost index funds — reinvested distributions eat capacity.
  6. Don't trade inside it — no loss offsetting, and capacity only returns next year.
  7. Spend it last — unlimited tax-free holding rewards patience.
