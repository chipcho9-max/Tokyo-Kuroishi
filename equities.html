AI & stocks

Being right about the technology is not the same as making money

Over the next 20–30 years, AI will probably rewrite large parts of the economy. This page accepts that premise. The investor's real question comes after it: will your capital actually capture any of that change? History says it is entirely possible to be right about the technology and still lose on the stock.

What this page is. The companies named below appear as structural explanation and case studies of the AI supply chain — not as a buy list. This site does not recommend individual securities and does not forecast returns for any company. On expected returns it gives you a framework you drive with your own assumptions, never a prediction. Listing status and business mix change — verify against primary sources before acting.
The central lesson

The thesis was right. The stock still lost.

In 1999 the claim "the internet will change everything" was completely correct. Cisco Systems, sitting at the centre of it, became the most valuable company on earth in March 2000. Internet traffic then grew exponentially for two more decades. The thesis worked. And yet:

CompanyIts position at the timeFall from peakTime to reclaim the peak
Cisco Systems March 2000: most valuable company in the world, core of internet infrastructure ≈ −85% ≈ 24 years (around 2024)
Microsoft End of 1999: undisputed ruler of the PC era ≈ −60% ≈ 17 years (2016) — then became one of the great compounders
Intel 2000: synonymous with semiconductors ≈ −80% Still not reclaimed a quarter-century later; lost the process lead, missed mobile and AI
Nokia 2007: roughly 40% of the global handset market ≈ −90% Not reclaimed. Lost the throne within a few years of the iPhone
Amazon 1999: an online bookseller ≈ −93% ≈ 9 years — then one of the best compounders in history

Approximate, price basis, rounded for readability.

Two things to take from this table. First, the people who lost were not the ones who backed the wrong technology — they were the ones who backed the right technology at the wrong price. Cisco kept earning and growing after 2000. The business was fine; the entry multiple was not. Second, the Amazon and Microsoft rows show that even the genuine winners put their holders through drawdowns of 60–93%. Whether you can sit through that is what decides your outcome.

An older precedent: the "Nifty Fifty". In the early 1970s, a set of US "one-decision" stocks — Coca-Cola, IBM, Xerox, Polaroid — traded at 50–90× earnings on the view that you could buy them and never sell. The judgment about company quality was often right. Investors who paid those prices still waited more than a decade to be rewarded, and some of those names (Polaroid, Kodak) eventually disappeared. A wonderful company and a wonderful investment are not the same thing.
Understand the structure

The AI value chain — where the money actually flows

"AI stocks" is not one thing. Each layer has a different margin structure, a different competitive position, and — most importantly — a different amount of expectation already priced in.

LayerRoleRepresentative companiesWhat matters for an investor
Chip design The AI accelerators themselves Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom (custom ASICs) Today's largest profit pool. Software (CUDA) is the real moat — and it is also the layer with the most expectation already in the price
Manufacturing Actually fabricating the chips TSMC, Samsung Foundry, Intel Foundry At the leading edge TSMC is close to a single point of failure for the whole industry. Geopolitical risk concentrates here
Equipment & materials The machines and inputs that make chips possible ASML (EUV monopoly), Tokyo Electron, Advantest, Disco, Shin-Etsu The "sells to whoever wins" layer, and the heart of the Japanese market's AI exposure. Still fully exposed to the semiconductor cycle
Memory High-bandwidth memory and storage SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron, Kioxia HBM is a genuine bottleneck for AI training. Memory has always been a violently cyclical commodity business, and that has not been repealed
Power & infrastructure Data centres, electricity, cooling Utilities, grid operators, cooling and power equipment The overlooked physical constraint. Power availability may end up limiting AI build-out more than chip supply
Cloud Renting the compute out Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta Simultaneously the biggest buyers of accelerators and, through their own silicon, the biggest long-term threat to the chip layer
Users of AI Everyone applying it to their own business Every industry Over 20–30 years this may be where most of the value lands. Historically the gains from a general-purpose technology accrue more to its users than its makers
Electricity and cars, as precedents. Electrification transformed the economy, but the biggest winners were not generator manufacturers — they were the manufacturers who used electricity to raise productivity. Over 2,000 companies entered the US auto industry; three survived. The scale of a transformation and the shareholder returns of the companies driving it are not the same variable.
Facts first

Japan

Japan's AI exposure is equipment and materials

The Japanese market's real AI leverage is not in finished products but in the machines and materials: Tokyo Electron, Advantest (test), Disco (dicing and grinding), Shin-Etsu and SUMCO (silicon wafers). They supply whichever chipmaker wins. Rapidus is attempting a leading-edge foundry, but judging that will take years, not quarters.

Korea

Korean investors already own this trade

Samsung and SK Hynix are such a large share of the KOSPI that simply holding a Korean index means already carrying enormous AI memory exposure. Adding individual AI names on top creates unintended double concentration. If you hold domestic equities, size any additional AI position with that in mind.

Expected returns

Decompose, don't predict

Nobody can tell you what an individual stock will return, and this site will not pretend otherwise. What it can give you is the decomposition that determines long-run returns. This is not a forecast — it is arithmetic on assumptions you choose:

Annual return ≈ (1 + earnings growth) × (1 + annualised change in P/E) − 1 + dividend yield
Long-run equity returns decompose almost entirely into these three: how much earnings grew, what multiple the market came to pay for those earnings, and the cash you collected along the way.

For a high-multiple stock, the second term is what bites. You can be right about the business and still watch a fall from 50× to 20× earnings subtract from your return every single year. Put your own assumptions in below.

That is the arithmetic this page exists to show. "This company will grow" can be true while the reward goes to the company rather than to you, because of the price you paid. Read it the other way round: to earn 10% a year from a 50× stock that settles at 20×, the business must compound earnings at 20.6% a year for a decade. Asking how often that actually happens is a better use of your time than watching the price.

Know the odds

Base rates for individual stocks

If you are going to pick stocks, it is worth knowing the starting probabilities. Hendrik Bessembinder's well-known research on US listed equities since 1926 found:

  • The majority of individual stocks underperformed Treasury bills over their lifetimes.
  • All of the market's net wealth creation came from roughly 4% of stocks. The other 96%, in aggregate, contributed approximately nothing.

Stock market returns are extremely concentrated in a few extreme winners. That creates a brutal asymmetry for the stock picker: a diversified index holder owns all of that 4% automatically, while someone holding ten names is more likely to miss the winners than to catch them.

And note: you already own Nvidia. If you contribute monthly to an S&P 500 or all-world index fund, you already hold Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon and TSMC at market weight. Better still, if those companies fade and others rise, the index swaps them for you without you doing anything. Given that the winners of 2050 may be companies that do not exist today, that property is worth more than it sounds.
In practice

If you're bullish on AI, how should a long-term investor act?

Wanting exposure to AI is not a mistake. The question is how you size it. This site's position is consistent: run it as a satellite around a core.

1. Measure what you already hold

Before buying anything, count the AI exposure already in your portfolio. The top of the S&P 500 is heavily AI-linked; a KOSPI holder carries a large Samsung + SK Hynix position by default. Know the existing position before adding.

2. Set the cap before you buy

Satellites together under 10–20% of the portfolio, no single company above 5%. The test is simple: if this went to zero, would my retirement date change? The probability of zero is higher than it feels.

3. Be wary of thematic ETFs

Thematic funds tend to launch when the theme is at peak attention, and charge an order of magnitude more than a broad index fund. Many historically underperformed their benchmark after launch. Check the inception date, the holdings and the fee before buying one.

4. Never stack leverage on concentration

Concentrated positions plus margin or leveraged ETFs turn an 80% drawdown from temporary into permanent. Cisco and Intel holders who simply held at least kept the option to decide. Leveraged holders did not.

5. Mind which account it sits in

NISA offers no loss offsetting. Putting a high-variance concentrated bet there forfeits the tax relief you would otherwise get. Sheltered accounts are for things likely to win over decades; speculative positions belong in the taxable account.

6. Keep the contribution schedule

Strong conviction is exactly when people abandon their schedule and go in all at once. But averaging in protects you most precisely when enthusiasm peaks. Remember Cisco in 1999.

20–30 years out

Scenarios, not predictions

Trying to call what the world looks like in 2050 is a poor basis for a portfolio. Building a plan that survives whichever scenario arrives is a much better one. At minimum, all three of these are plausible:

A. Today's winners keep winning

Software lock-in and scale economics hold, and the current leaders dominate for two more decades. Index investors capture it automatically. Stock pickers are rewarded too — provided they did not overpay at the entry.

B. The leadership changes hands

A new compute architecture, or hyperscalers' own silicon, shifts the profit pool to a different layer — exactly the Intel and Nokia precedent. The index follows along automatically; this is where concentration gets hurt most.

C. The gains go to the users

The technology diffuses, competition commoditises it, and the profits spread to the broad set of companies that used AI to get more productive — the electrification path. In this scenario, owning the whole market is ironically the optimal AI trade.

Exactly one approach does acceptably well in all three. Owning the broad market, for a long time, without interruption. Layering an AI conviction on top of that is fine — but it belongs on top of the foundation, not underneath it. Since the winners of 2050 may be companies that do not yet exist, "owning whoever wins" is structurally a stronger position than "picking the winner".
