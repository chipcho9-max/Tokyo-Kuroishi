Markets

Three markets, three masterclasses

The US market shows what decades of compounding can deliver. Japan shows what happens when you pay any price for it. Korea shows why governance and shareholder returns are not footnotes. A long-horizon investor should understand all three — especially if you live in one and invest in another.

United States

The compounding benchmark

The S&P 500's long-run average total return is roughly 10% a year in nominal terms (about 7% after inflation), measured from 1926 through 2024 with dividends reinvested. That average hides violent decade-to-decade variation — the 2000s returned roughly zero, the 2010s were exceptional — but the source of the return is not mysterious: over long periods it is earnings growth plus dividends, with valuation changes adding noise that matters less the longer you hold.

Why has the US compounded so well? Deep capital markets, relentless corporate focus on shareholder returns, global revenue reach, and an unmatched pipeline of new dominant companies. Be careful with the conclusion, though: "the US always wins" is a historical observation, not a law. It is a strong reason to make US equities a core holding; it is a weaker reason to hold nothing else. Many long-horizon investors pair a US core with a global ex-US fund precisely because nobody gets a guarantee.

Japan

The world's most important cautionary tale — read carefully

In December 1989 the Nikkei 225 peaked near 39,000 at the height of an asset bubble, trading at price-earnings multiples several times those of other developed markets. It then fell for over a decade and did not close above its 1989 peak until February 2024 — a 34-year round trip. This is the scenario every long-term investor must be able to answer for, and there are three honest lessons in it:

  • Price matters even for the patient. "Stocks always come back" failed for a lump sum invested at 60–70× earnings. Buying an entire market at extreme valuation can consume your whole investing lifetime. Time dilutes normal valuations; it does not rescue absurd ones.
  • Concentration in one country is a real risk. In 1989 Japan was ~40% of world market value and its permanence felt as obvious as anything feels today. A Japanese investor who also owned world equities experienced a completely different three decades than one who owned only the Nikkei.
  • Steady buying changes the story. The 34-year figure describes a single unlucky lump sum at the exact top. An investor who dollar-cost averaged into the Japanese market through the 1990s and 2000s accumulated shares at depressed prices for years and returned to profit far sooner — and with dividends reinvested, the broader TOPIX recovered meaningfully earlier than the price-only Nikkei story implies. Bad decades punish lump-sum tops hardest and steady savers least.

Modern Japan is a different market: the Tokyo Stock Exchange has been pressuring companies trading below book value to improve capital efficiency, buybacks and dividends have risen sharply, and the Nikkei finally made new all-time highs in 2024. For a Japanese resident, the practical takeaways live on the Accounts & Tax page and the NISA guide: the new NISA makes decades-long compounding in US or global index funds tax-free, and the yen/dollar exchange rate — not stock picking — will be the biggest swing factor you actually feel.

Korea

The governance lesson — and the 서학개미 wave

Korea's economy produced world-class companies, yet the KOSPI spent most of 2011–2020 trapped in a sideways range investors nicknamed the "Box-pi". The gap between corporate quality and shareholder returns has a name — the "Korea discount" — and well-understood causes: low dividend payout ratios, complex chaebol cross-shareholdings, controlling-family incentives that don't align with minority shareholders, and heavy index concentration in cyclical exporters (semiconductors above all).

The lesson for a long-term investor is subtle and important: GDP growth and stock returns are not the same thing. What compounds for shareholders is earnings per share that is actually returned or reinvested on their behalf. Korea's 2024 "Corporate Value-up Program", modeled on Japan's reforms, is an attempt to close exactly this gap — worth watching, because if payout culture genuinely changes, cheap markets can re-rate dramatically.

Meanwhile millions of Korean retail investors — nicknamed 서학개미 ("Western-learning ants") — moved savings into US stocks directly. The instinct (own global winners, escape the discount) fits a long-horizon plan well, but the execution often doesn't: concentrated single-stock bets, leveraged ETFs, and frequent trading are the opposite of what made US returns attractive in the first place. A Korean investor holding a broad US index fund for 20 years also needs to understand the 22% overseas capital-gains tax, 15% US dividend withholding, and the US estate-tax exposure — all covered in Accounts & Tax.

The cross-border question

Holding US stocks in yen or won terms

If you live in Japan or Korea and buy US stocks, you own two exposures: American companies and the dollar. Over a single year, the exchange rate can easily move 10–20% and dominate your statement. Over 20–30 years, equity returns have historically dwarfed net currency drift — but the currency still decides how smooth the ride feels and what your money buys at home when you finally sell.

The practical defaults

  • Unhedged is the common long-horizon default. Hedging costs roughly the interest-rate differential every year, which compounds into a real drag over decades — and for a JPY or KRW investor that differential has often been substantial.
  • Dollar-cost averaging averages the currency too. Buying monthly for decades means you buy dollars cheap and expensive in turn, rather than betting your whole stake on one exchange rate.
  • Match currency to future spending. Money you will spend in yen or won within a few years doesn't belong in unhedged dollar assets at all.

What not to do

  • Don't switch between hedged and unhedged funds based on exchange-rate forecasts — that is currency trading with extra steps.
  • Don't judge a 30-year plan by one year of currency pain; a weak home currency flatters your returns, a strong one hides them, and neither changes the underlying businesses.
  • Don't forget that your salary, pension, and home already tie most of your life to your home currency — some foreign-currency assets are a diversifier, not a risk.
