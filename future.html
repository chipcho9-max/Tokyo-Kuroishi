The next thirty years

The exciting layer runs on a boring one

Intelligence gets the headlines. Underneath it sits a layer nobody writes about: electricity, transformers, cooling water, fibre, physical security, and the people who keep all of it running at three in the morning. That layer is not optional, and unlike software it cannot be scaled by spending alone. This page is about the constraints — and about the difference between a sector mattering and a share price rewarding you for noticing.

What this page is, and is not. The constraints described below are documented — grid queues, published capacity figures, stated build plans. The step from "this will matter" to "this stock will pay" is not, and this site does not take it. Companies appear as structural explanation, never as a buy list, and no return is forecast for any of them. Verify every figure against its primary source before acting on it.
The binding constraint

It is electricity, and it has a queue

The head of the company selling the chips said it plainly on an earnings call: if we had more compute, we could generate more profitable tokens. Compute is the constraint. But compute has a constraint of its own, and it is not fabs — it is power, and the land and grid connections that power arrives through.

Two features of that demand make it harder to serve than the raw number suggests. It concentrates. Electric vehicles spread load across a country; a gigawatt-class cluster lands in one county. And it arrives faster than grids are built — connection requests queue, planning permission takes years, and the transformers and turbines have order books measured in the same units.

That is the sentence to keep. Whatever happens to any model company, the concrete, copper and generating capacity have to exist first, and they take years that money cannot compress.

The maintenance layer

What has to be kept running, and who keeps it

A useful way to think about the next few decades is to ask which parts of the system fail if neglected for a week. Those are the parts that get maintained forever, and maintenance revenue behaves very differently from product revenue: smaller, duller, and far more repeatable.

LayerWhat it actually involvesWhat it connects to
Generation Gas turbines for firm capacity, nuclear including the small-reactor projects now being signed against data-centre demand, and renewables plus the storage that makes them dispatchable The utilities, turbine makers and EPC contractors — old industries with long order backlogs and, usually, regulated returns
Transmission and equipment Transformers, switchgear, high-voltage cable, substations. The bottleneck that rarely makes headlines: lead times for large transformers are measured in years Grid equipment makers. Japan and Korea both have real positions here, in heavy electrical machinery and cable
The building Data-centre shells, power distribution, and cooling — increasingly liquid, because the chips now exceed what air can carry away Construction, HVAC and thermal-management suppliers, plus water rights, which is becoming a genuine siting constraint
Security Both kinds. Physical access control at sites that are now national infrastructure, and the cyber layer covered in AI & Stocks Security vendors and integrators. Note that Japan's Cyber Response Capability Enhancement Act takes effect 1 October 2026 and is aimed squarely at critical infrastructure operators
People Electricians, linesmen, HVAC technicians, site engineers. The least discussed constraint of all, and the one that cannot be solved by a purchase order Staffing and industrial services, and a labour-cost pressure that runs through every line above
The honest caveat, which matters more than the list. Every business above is capital-intensive: heavy fixed assets, long project cycles, real execution risk, and in the utility cases a regulator who decides the return. These are not software margins, and historically many of these industries have been poor long-run investments precisely because demand was reliable enough to attract competitors and capital. Reliable demand is not the same as pricing power. Ask which of these businesses can raise prices, not just which will be busy.
Space

The industry stopped being unbuyable — which starts a harder argument

For years the honest answer about SpaceX was that you could not own it, so it did not belong in a plan. That changed on 12 June 2026, when SpaceX listed on Nasdaq under SPCX in the largest IPO on record: priced at $135, first day closing near $161, a market capitalisation around $2 trillion. Since February 2026 the same entity also contains xAI, so one listed company now spans launch, satellite internet and frontier AI models.

The question this creates is not whether the engineering is extraordinary. It is the question this site asks about everything: at that price, what does the business have to deliver for the buyer to do well? Put your own assumptions into the decomposition tool and see what they require. A great company bought at a great price and a great company bought at any price are the two different stories that Japan in 1989 and Cisco in 2000 already tell on this site.

There is also a record worth knowing before buying the theme rather than the company. The wave of pure-play space companies that reached the market through SPACs in 2020–21 was, for shareholders, close to a catalogue of disasters — several fell more than 90%, and some no longer exist. The technology thesis was not wrong. The vehicles were early, unprofitable, and bought at prices that required everything to go right.

One more practical point that is new: SpaceX now files. Revenue, margins, segment detail and risk factors are public for the first time. Anyone forming a view should read the filings rather than the mythology — the company has spent two decades being discussed without numbers, and the numbers are now available.

Sustainability

SDGs: separate the engineering from the label

There are two quite different things wearing the same word. One is an engineering and industrial reality: grids are being rebuilt, heat is being electrified, efficiency is being retrofitted, and water is becoming a siting constraint for the data centres in the section above. The other is a labelling industry — funds, ratings and scores.

The record of the second is genuinely mixed. Broad ESG funds have often charged more than plain index funds while holding much the same large-cap companies; ratings from different providers frequently disagree about the same firm; and regulators in several jurisdictions have had to act against overstated environmental claims. None of that means the underlying transition is fake. It means the label is not the investment, and a fee paid for a label is a fee paid for nothing — which the cost section shows compounds against you for thirty years.

Where the physical work is

Transmission and distribution equipment, transformers, cabling, storage, heat pumps, insulation, grid software, water treatment. Unglamorous, capital-intensive, frequently regulated — and the actual content of the transition.

What to check before paying for a theme

The expense ratio against a plain index fund, what the fund actually holds when you open the factsheet, how often constituents change, and whether you already own most of it through a broad index. Very often the honest answer is that you do.

A long horizon is the right frame for this transition, because it takes decades. That is an argument for patience, not an argument for paying more.

One person, several industries

The Musk map: what each business does, and what it touches

Elon Musk's companies are unusually useful as a teaching example, because between them they sit on almost every layer this page describes — energy, compute, connectivity, robotics and launch. Here is the map as it currently stands, and what a long-horizon investor can and cannot actually buy.

Group structures change, and this one has changed twice in the past year. Now that SpaceX is listed, verify the current structure in its filings rather than in coverage.

The risk that is specific to admiring a founder. Concentrating capital around one person means one health event, one distraction, one reputational rupture or one regulatory fight is a portfolio event. It also means several of your holdings can be correlated in ways an index will not warn you about — owning Tesla and SpaceX is not diversification, it is the same bet twice. Size any of this as a satellite, and be honest that conviction and concentration are the same thing viewed from different sides. Admiring what someone is building is a good reason to read the filings. It is not a position size.

What to do with all this

Four things that survive being wrong about the details

  • Check what you already own before adding anything. A broad global index fund already holds the utilities, the grid-equipment makers, the security platforms and the hyperscalers. For most readers the honest answer to "how do I get exposure to this" is that you have it.
  • Distinguish "will be busy" from "will earn." Reliable demand attracts capital and competitors. Ask which businesses in the chain can raise prices, and be suspicious of any answer that assumes a commodity supplier keeps a windfall margin.
  • Treat thematic funds as a fee question first. Energy-transition, space and AI-infrastructure ETFs turn over holdings heavily and cost more than a plain index. Open the factsheet and look at what is actually inside.
  • Keep the schedule. None of this changes the mechanism that does the work: regular contributions into a broad, low-cost core, held through the decades in which the story changes three times. The calculator is still the page that matters most.
