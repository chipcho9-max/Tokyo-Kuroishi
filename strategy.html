Strategy

A portfolio you can hold for 30 years

The best long-term strategy is not the one with the highest theoretical return. It is the best strategy you will actually stick with through four bear markets, two job changes, and ten thousand scary headlines. Simplicity is not a compromise — it is the feature.

Step 1

Build around a broad index core

For most decade-scale investors, the core holding is a low-cost, broad-market index fund — an S&P 500 or total-market fund for US exposure, optionally an all-world fund for global spread. The case rests on three durable facts:

  • Most professionals fail to beat the index over long periods. The SPIVA scorecards find that over 15–20 year windows, roughly 90% of actively managed US equity funds underperform their benchmark, largely because of costs.
  • Returns are concentrated in a few big winners you can't identify in advance. Owning the whole market guarantees you own them at full weight.
  • Index funds are administratively unbreakable. No manager retires, no thesis expires, nothing needs your attention for 30 years. Boring is the point.

Individual stocks and thematic funds can live around the core as satellites — sized so that a total loss in any one of them changes nothing about when you retire. A common rule: satellites all together stay under 10–20% of the portfolio, and no single company exceeds ~5%.

Step 2

Dollar-cost average — automatically

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) means investing a fixed amount on a fixed schedule — every payday, every month — regardless of prices or news. For someone saving out of income it is simply the natural way to invest, and it quietly solves the three hardest problems in investing:

  • Timing: you never make a single all-in decision at what might be a top (the mistake at the heart of the Japan 1989 story).
  • Behavior: automation keeps you buying in crashes, when buying feels worst and matters most — falling prices raise the share count your fixed amount buys.
  • Currency (for Japan/Korea-based investors): monthly buying averages your dollar purchase price too, instead of betting everything on one exchange rate.
Got a lump sum instead? Historically, investing a windfall immediately has beaten spreading it out about two times in three, because markets rise more often than they fall. But the third of the time it loses can be brutal at the start of your journey. Splitting a large windfall over 6–18 months is a reasonable price for a plan you'll stick to. What matters is deciding the schedule up front — and following it mechanically. Model both in the calculator.

One more habit multiplies DCA's power: raise the contribution every time your income rises. An annual 3–5% step-up, automated, can add hundreds of thousands to a 30-year outcome — the calculator has a field for exactly this.

A popular variant

Dividend growth investing

Many 20–30 year investors — especially those planning to live on their portfolio without selling shares — favor dividend growth strategies: owning companies (or index funds of companies) with long records of raising their payouts year after year.

The genuine advantages

  • A rising income stream you can eventually spend without timing share sales.
  • Payout discipline filters for durable, cash-generative businesses.
  • Psychologically easier to hold through crashes — the dividends keep arriving even while prices fall, which is exactly when reinvested dividends buy the most shares.

The honest caveats

  • Total return is what compounds; a dividend paid is not free money, and dividend strategies are not automatically safer or better.
  • Taxes bite every year. Dividends are taxed as they are paid (and for Japanese/Korean holders of US stocks, 10–15% is withheld in the US), while unrealized capital gains compound untaxed until you sell.
  • Chasing the highest yields selects for broken businesses about to cut. Dividend growth, not dividend size, is the quality signal.

A reasonable synthesis: treat dividend growth as a tilt on a broad core rather than a replacement for it, and hold it in the most tax-sheltered account available to you (see Accounts & Tax).

Step 3

Set an allocation that matches the horizon

Asset allocation — how much you hold in stocks versus bonds and cash — matters more than any fund choice. The long-horizon logic: volatility is the risk that fades with time, inflation is the risk that grows with it, so the further away the goal, the more the portfolio should lean toward stocks.

Years until you need the moneyCommon equity rangeReasoning
20–30+ years 80–100% stocks Decades to recover from any bear market; inflation is the enemy. Bonds are optional ballast for your behavior, not for return.
10–20 years 60–85% stocks Still firmly growth territory; begin adding high-quality bonds as the goal approaches its final decade.
Under 10 years 30–60% stocks Sequence risk becomes real — a crash just before you start spending hurts most. Glide down gradually, not in one move.

The right number inside each range is set by your stomach, not a formula: choose the allocation whose worst year — roughly half the equity share, as a percentage loss — you could watch happen without selling. An investor who holds 80% stocks through a crash beats the one who planned 100% and capitulated at the bottom.

Step 4

Rebalance on rules, review once a year

  • Rebalance when drift exceeds a band (e.g. an 80/20 target becomes 85/15 or 75/25), or simply once a year on a fixed date. Both work; neither requires forecasts.
  • Rebalance with new money first. Directing contributions toward whatever is underweight avoids selling — and avoids realizing taxable gains, which matters enormously outside sheltered accounts.
  • Hold an annual review, not a daily vigil. Once a year: confirm the allocation, raise the contribution with your income, check that each account's tax wrapper is being filled (see the checklists), and re-read your one-page investment policy. Then close the app.
The short list

Mistakes that break 30-year plans

1. Selling in a crash

The single most expensive act in retail investing. It converts a temporary decline into a permanent loss and usually misses the recovery days that deliver most of the rebound. Defense: allocation you can stomach, cash emergency fund, automation.

2. Concentration creep

One winning stock, one hot sector, or one country (including your own — and including the US) quietly becomes half the portfolio. Bulls feel like conviction; the Nikkei chart shows the exit. Cap positions and rebalance.

3. Performance chasing

Buying whatever returned most in the last 3 years is a systematic way to buy high. Fund flows show investors doing this in every cycle — it is the mechanical cause of the behavior gap.

4. Leverage and its ETF cousins

Margin turns temporary declines into forced permanent sales. Leveraged ETFs decay through daily rebalancing and are not designed to be held for decades, whatever the backtest says. A 30-year plan needs to survive every path, not just the average one.

5. Ignoring the tax wrapper

Investing in a taxable account while a Roth IRA, NISA, ISA or 연금저축 sits empty donates decades of compounding to the tax office. Fill the sheltered space first — it's the highest risk-free return available to you.

6. Interrupting compounding for "a better entry"

Going to cash to wait out uncertainty feels prudent and has a dreadful record — uncertainty never resolves cleanly, and re-entry never feels safe. The plan is the schedule; the schedule is the plan.

Putting it together

Three example blueprints

Illustrative structures, not recommendations — fund availability and the right wrapper depend on your country (see Accounts & Tax).

The one-decision portfolio

100% — one all-world or target-date index fund.

Maximum simplicity, automatic diversification and (for target-date funds) automatic glide path. Almost impossible to fiddle with. For many people this is the best portfolio they will ever own.

The US-core classic

70% S&P 500 / total US market · 20% developed & emerging ex-US · 10% bonds or cash-like.

The default shape for a 20–30 year horizon with a US-growth conviction but a Japan-1989 insurance policy. Rebalanced annually.

The dividend-growth tilt

60% broad US/global core · 30% dividend-growth index fund · 10% bonds.

For investors who want a rising income stream in the drawdown decades and accept the tax drag along the way. Best kept inside sheltered accounts.
